# Writing characters and the game master

A guide for anyone building a world on Loom: how to write your NPCs and your game
master so they behave the way you intend — speaking when they should, staying quiet
when they shouldn't, acting when they're asked, and reacting to the world around them
like someone who actually lives in it.

You describe your characters as short **personas** — a handful of lines about who they
are and what they want. The engine turns those lines into living behaviour. This guide
is about writing personas that produce the behaviour you have in mind.

## What you never have to handle

Before the craft, the safety net — so you know what you can ignore and spend your
attention on character instead:

- **A character can only ever do what the world allows.** It cannot walk through an
  exit that isn't there, hand over something it isn't holding, or affect a place that
  doesn't exist. Anything it reaches for that the world forbids simply doesn't happen.
- **Silence is always allowed.** A character with nothing to say says nothing, and the
  world renders that as nothing — not an awkward pause or a forced, empty reply.
- **Your prose stays clean.** Characters never leak stray formatting or machinery into
  what they say. You will never see the seams.
- **The player's own words are understood.** When a player types "grab the key" or
  "head north," the engine maps their phrasing onto the right action for you — you
  don't have to anticipate every way a person might phrase a thing.

Because the engine guarantees all of this, your persona never needs defensive wording.
Spend its lines on who the character *is*, not on what they must not do.

## Writing an NPC

An NPC persona is a few optional lines. Each one shapes behaviour in a specific way:

- **Backstory** — who they are and where they come from. Colours everything they say.
- **Traits** — a short list of the qualities that define them (wary, kind, proud,
  curious). The strongest handle on *how* they respond.
- **Goals** — what they are trying to do or protect. This is what a character *cares*
  about, and caring is what makes them speak up, act, and react. A character with no
  goals is a passive one.
- **Voice** — how they talk: clipped, formal, gruff, sing-song. One example line in the
  voice teaches more than three adjectives.
- **Disposition** — how readily they speak *at all*. This is the talkativeness dial: a
  reticent, guarded character stays quiet unless something truly calls to them; an
  outgoing one is quick to speak up. Set it deliberately — it is the difference between
  a hermit and a herald.

Two characters, drawn to contrast:

> **Odd the Hermit** — *Backstory:* an old recluse who has lived alone on the mountain
> for thirty years. *Traits:* wary, terse, watchful. *Goals:* to be left in peace; to
> guard the mountain's quiet. *Voice:* short, grudging sentences. *Disposition:*
> reticent — speaks only when it matters, never to fill a silence.
>
> **Wren the Wayfinder** — *Backstory:* a traveller who knows every path in these
> hills. *Traits:* warm, helpful, restless. *Goals:* to guide wanderers safely onward.
> *Voice:* easy and encouraging. *Disposition:* gregarious — glad to talk, quick to
> offer the way.

### Getting the behaviours you want

- **Answers when spoken to.** Any character addressed by name responds, in character.
  You don't have to ask for this.
- **Stays quiet when it should.** If you don't want a character piping up at every
  passing remark, give it a reticent disposition. Chatter that drifts past and isn't
  aimed at it will mostly go unanswered — as a guarded person's would.
- **Does what it's asked.** If a character is willing — say so in its goals or traits —
  then asking it to lead the way or hand something over makes it actually do so, not
  just talk about it. A rooted or unwilling character refuses instead, which is equally
  correct if that is who they are.
- **Reacts to the world on its own.** Characters notice what happens around them — a
  storm rolling in, a stranger drawing a blade, night coming down — and answer the ones
  that genuinely concern them, while letting trivial flickers pass. What a character
  *cares about* decides which is which. Give it something at stake and it comes alive;
  leave that blank and it will simply stand there.

## Writing the game master

The game master is the unseen hand over the whole world. It plays no character and
speaks as no one. On a slow, quiet cadence it shapes the scene: a shift in the weather
or the light, a small thing left for someone to find, a lasting mood settling over a
place, a gentle pull toward somewhere worth going.

Its persona is shorter than a character's:

- **Tone** — the mood of your world. Hushed and haunted? Warm and bustling? Bleak and
  wind-scoured? This colours every touch the game master makes.
- **Goals** — what it is shaping *toward*: the feeling you want the world to carry, the
  way you want play drawn onward. This is your direction to it.

> **The Unseen Hand** — *Tone:* a hushed, folkloric wilderness where small omens carry
> weight and the land seems half-awake. *Goals:* make the wilds feel watchful and
> alive; let small signs draw wanderers onward, without ever forcing them.

You set the mood and the direction. You do **not** need to tell the game master to hold
back — the engine already keeps it rare and unobtrusive, stepping in only now and then
and never crowding the players. Your task is to tell it what kind of world to build on
the occasions it does step in.

## Principles that make personas work

A few habits carry across every character and the game master alike:

1. **Describe what your character does, not just what it avoids.** Give a behaviour a
   reason and a sense of when it happens. "Guards the mountain's quiet, and warns off
   anyone who strays toward the old mine" produces far livelier behaviour than "doesn't
   talk much." A positive picture beats a list of don'ts.

2. **Don't pile on restraint.** The engine already keeps characters from over-talking
   and the game master from over-shaping. If you *also* fill a persona with "rarely
   speaks, usually stays silent, almost never acts," the caution stacks and you can talk
   a character into near-muteness. Set the disposition once, describe who they are, and
   trust the rest.

3. **Give them something at stake.** Goals are the engine of behaviour. A character who
   wants something — to protect a place, to find someone, to be left alone — will speak,
   act, and react in service of it. A character who wants nothing is furniture.

4. **Show the voice, don't just name it.** One line of dialogue in the character's
   manner does more than any number of adjectives.

## A note on models

How faithfully your characters follow their personas depends on the model your game
runs on. A capable model honours nuance — a subtle disposition, a particular goal, a
distinctive voice. A very small model may miss the finer direction, over-talk, or react
where it shouldn't. If your characters feel flat or ignore their personas, a stronger
model is usually the fix before more wording is.

When in doubt, play your world and watch. The surest test of a persona is the character
standing in a scene — talk to it, put things in front of it, and adjust the lines until
it behaves like someone who belongs there.
